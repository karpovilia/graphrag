\begin{abstract}
GraphRAG pipelines build entity-relation graphs from text and use them for retrieval and question answering, but in practice they operate as a black box: extraction noise, fragmented entities, and unstable clustering silently distort the evidence behind an answer. Diagnosis becomes harder still when the corpus evolves, because the graph is rebuilt incrementally and an error in one answer is usually the surface symptom of a cascade accumulated across pipeline stages and updates.

We present GraphCraft, an interactive system that makes change the unit of analysis and the journal of changes its training data. A bi-temporal model separates transaction time, when a fact entered the graph, from event time, when the fact holds in the world. One visual language of change covers three axes: a heatmap timeline whose brush diffs any period, edits whose effect ripples through incremental recomputation, and queries that light up their evidence subgraph with provenance down to source spans. Curation agents propose provenance-linked suggestions the user accepts or rejects, and any slice of the edit journal can be compiled into a reusable skill, built at three price points and previewed on the live graph before adoption. We demonstrate the workflow on a podcast corpus and an evolving news corpus and release the system as an open-source, offline-deployable platform.
\footnote{Code and offline deployment: \url{https://github.com/karpovilia/graphrag}; demo video: \url{https://vimeo.com/1141894235}.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Graph-based Retrieval-Augmented Generation (GraphRAG) induces an entity--relation graph over a document collection and uses this structure to retrieve context for question answering (QA)~\cite{edge2025localglobalgraphrag}. By explicitly structuring entities, relations, and communities, GraphRAG provides an externalized representation that supports retrieval beyond the model's immediate context window. Because the pipeline is not optimized end-to-end, intermediate outputs become fixed inputs for later stages, so local inaccuracies persist: entity fragmentation, spurious relations, or unstable clustering accumulate across stages and distort both graph structure and retrieved evidence, a phenomenon we call cascading error. In black-box settings these failures are hard to localize: answers stay fluent while the reasoning path is opaque.

Moreover, no two induced graphs fail alike: the corpus's domain, purpose, and language dictate which errors dominate, so every knowledge graph effectively demands its own processing toolchain. Highly inflective languages, for instance, fragment entities into inflected forms and abbreviations unless dedicated preprocessing absorbs them; elsewhere the dominant noise is spurious relations or plausible-but-wrong communities. Rather than hard-coding a toolchain for one corpus, we build curation agents that adapt: they learn the curator's rules from the edit journal itself. Diagnosis becomes harder still when the corpus is not static: a correct entity can fragment only after a later batch, a community can reorganize between refreshes, and an automatic update can invalidate a fact that was correct. Disentangling these effects requires a bi-temporal representation, long standard in temporal databases~\cite{jensen1999temporal, kulkarni2012sql2011}: transaction time records when the system learned a fact, event time records when the fact holds in the world, and a graph stamped with both axes lets an extraction error, wrong on the event axis, be told apart from a genuine change in the corpus, new on the transaction axis.

We introduce \textbf{GraphCraft}, which turns GraphRAG from a black-box pipeline into a transparent, steerable workflow built on one stance: change is the unit of analysis, and the journal of changes is training data. Everything else in the system is a projection of this stance: the delta grammar is the language of change, the bi-temporal model is their storage, the activity timeline their navigation, curation their production, and compiled skills their reuse. Four contributions follow. (i)~A \textbf{bi-temporal data model} separates the cumulative transaction axis T$'$ from the event axis T and lets facts extracted from text take their natural temporal form: timeless, half-open, or point. (ii)~A single \textbf{delta grammar} of born, dead, persisted, moved-community, and invalidated facts is rendered uniformly across three axes of change: time, edits, and queries. (iii)~An \textbf{activity-heatmap timeline} selects a period rather than an instant and shows where much vs.\ little happened, so a diff over any window surfaces exactly what the graph gained, lost, or reorganized. (iv)~An \textbf{agentic curation layer}: agents propose provenance-linked suggestions the user accepts or rejects, and any journal slice can be compiled into a reusable skill, built at three price points and previewed on the live graph before adoption. The toolchain thus adapts to the domain, purpose, and language of the corpus instead of being fixed in advance. Unlike a visual graph editor, GraphCraft treats edits as first-class pipeline events: every correction updates the derived artifacts used by retrieval, every automatic change remains reviewable, and repeated corrections can be compiled into reusable skills.

The target users are practitioners and domain experts who treat GraphRAG as an evolving system rather than a fixed component. The core loop is ask, inspect, edit, re-ask: incremental recomputation refreshes only the affected set of an edit, structural updates landing immediately and summary and embedding refreshes completing in the background, so the re-asked question reflects the repair without a pipeline re-run, while layers and entity-type filters keep an otherwise unreadable graph at a visual volume a human can take in. Nothing is asserted without lineage: every answer and every agent proposal traces back to source spans and to the reasoning path that produced it.

\section{Related Work}
GraphRAG builds an induced entity--relation graph over a collection to support retrieval and QA~\cite{edge2025localglobalgraphrag}. Our work connects to four areas.

\textbf{Graph-enhanced LLM reasoning.}
Think-on-Graph and its extensions~\cite{sun2024thinkongraph, ma2024thinkongraph2, sun2024thinkongraph3} guide inference via graph traversal and study faithfulness, typically assuming an available, sufficiently clean graph. We instead target failure modes of automatically induced, evolving graphs and provide interactive curation that persists and immediately affects downstream retrieval.

\textbf{GraphRAG and automatic graph induction.}
Microsoft GraphRAG~\cite{edge2025localglobalgraphrag} induces a graph by LLM extraction, then applies community detection and hierarchical summarization for local-to-global retrieval; related work explores alternative chunking and retrieval~\cite{sarthi2024raptorrecursiveabstractiveprocessing, zhong2025mixofgranularityoptimizechunkinggranularity} and couples KGs with LLM QA~\cite{sen-etal-2023-knowledge, toroghi2025rightrightreasonslarge}; iText2KG~\cite{lairgi2024itext2kg} and HippoRAG~\cite{gutierrez2024hipporag} build the knowledge graph incrementally as documents arrive. All of these treat induction as an automated stage with limited support for human-facing debugging and persistent corrections.

\textbf{Temporal knowledge graphs and agent memory.}
Temporal-KG reasoning, e.g.\ SiMFy~\cite{liu2023simfy}, forecasts future facts on curated benchmarks, a non-interactive task orthogonal to ours. Zep/Graphiti~\cite{rasmussen2025zep} maintains a bi-temporal agent-memory graph with automatic fact invalidation but exposes no visualization or curation. Such an engine can serve as one pluggable temporal builder beneath our diagnosis layer; our system makes its structure, and its wrong invalidations, visible and human-correctable.

\textbf{Interactive diagnosis and graph tools.}
XGraphRAG~\cite{wang2025xgraphraginteractivevisualanalysis} is the closest system: visual analysis that traces suspicious retrievals through the pipeline, yet read-only over a fixed graph. AGENTiGraph~\cite{zhao2025agentigraph} translates chat intents into operations over manually curated KGs, whereas our agents curate automatically induced artifacts and stay accountable to a provenance-linked journal. Rule induction from user edits descends from programming by demonstration: FlashFill~\cite{gulwani2011flashfill} synthesizes transforms from examples, Wrangler~\cite{kandel2011wrangler} suggests data-cleaning steps from an edit history, and Voyager~\cite{wang2023voyager} accretes reusable skills in an LLM agent. GraphCraft differs in inducing graph-curation rules from a provenance-linked journal, materializing each rule at three cost tiers, and previewing its effect on the live graph before adoption. Neo4j Bloom~\cite{neo4j_bloom} and Cytoscape~\cite{shannon2003cytoscape} support navigation and editing, and the Vistorian~\cite{bach2015networkcube} and Gephi~\cite{bastian2009gephi} offer timelines of evolving networks, but none model GraphRAG pipelines or separate transaction from event time. Nor does stacking Graphiti under Bloom close the gap. An edit in Bloom persists and will affect subsequent retrieval, but nothing recomputes the derived artifacts that retrieval actually consumes: summaries, community assignments, and embeddings keep describing the pre-edit graph, and no affected set tells the user what went stale. An invalidation in Graphiti, in turn, has no review surface. The missing piece is the interactive loop, where a human edit propagates forward through the derived artifacts to the answer and an automatic change propagates backward to a human question; that loop is what this system contributes.

\section{Implementation}
GraphCraft is a modular pipeline behind an interactive web interface. Every stage, from chunking and extraction to clustering, summarization, retrieval, and reasoning, is a plugin: builders (Microsoft GraphRAG~\cite{edge2025localglobalgraphrag}, LightRAG-style profiling~\cite{guo2024lightrag}), cleaners (threshold pruning, morphological deduplication, two-mode projection~\cite{batagelj2013bibliographic}), clusterers (Leiden~\cite{Traag_2019}, modularity~\cite{Newman_2006}, Bayan~\cite{Aref_2024}), and reasoners can be swapped and compared. Every node and edge carries a layer (chunk, entity, community, topic) and provenance back to a document span. Figure~\ref{fig:stack} summarizes the stack and marks what is new relative to a conventional graph-tool pipeline.

\textbf{Bi-temporal core and delta grammar.} Transaction time, stored as \texttt{tx\_from} and \texttt{tx\_to}, records when a fact entered the graph and is cumulative; event time, stored as \texttt{valid\_from} and \texttt{valid\_to}, records when the fact holds in the world, in timeless, half-open, or point form. One grammar of born, dead, persisted, moved-community, and invalidated facts is rendered identically across three axes: a diff between two periods classifies every node and edge into these buckets, an edit animates its affected set under a latency badge, and a query lights its evidence subgraph while the rest is dimmed. The timeline scrubber is a row of weekly cells coloured by activity volume; its brush selects a period, and scrubbing the cumulative axis replays how the graph filled in week by week.

\textbf{Focused views.} Induced graphs are dense, so the toolbar exposes entity-type chips: showing one or several node types at once with a consistent colour key collapses an unreadable tangle into a legible map. Latent two-mode structure, such as entities sharing chunks, can be materialized as derived edges under hub-deflating normalization~\cite{newman2001collaboration} and toggled on demand.

\textbf{Curation and revert.} Operations are issued from the canvas: a node opens a fact card with per-fact verify, fix, and delete actions drilling to the source span; a chunk node shows the inverse view, its text next to every entity extracted from it; merge is a drag of one node onto another, and split partitions the source documents across the resulting nodes. Every operation lands in a provenance-linked journal, and an incremental engine refreshes only the affected summaries, embeddings, and community assignments, so downstream QA updates without reprocessing the corpus. When an automatic update soft-invalidates an edge, the invalidation carries provenance and can be reverted, the failure mode a fully automatic backend cannot expose. Curation is also multi-user, in the manner of a collaborative editor: one graph variant is one shared document, every signed-in user edits it directly, and the server serializes operations in arrival order, without merging concurrent edits; a teammate's edit arrives over a socket and ripples through the same delta grammar, signed with its author. When two curators race on the same node the later operation prevails, but both land in the journal, so the earlier author can see what happened and reapply their edit.

\textbf{Journal-to-skill compilation.} The journal doubles as training data. The user selects their last $n$ operations; each is a structured witness that records the operation type, the nodes it touched with their types and layers, and the outcome. Rule induction runs three passes over these witnesses with the same pluggable LLM backend the pipeline uses for extraction: the first pass articulates the rule the curator appears to follow, the second applies that rule to the rest of the graph and proposes candidate operations, and the third verifies every candidate against the rule and grounds it in the live graph, dropping those whose targets no longer exist. The resulting skill is scoped to the whole graph, one layer, or one node type, and a wizard builds it at three price points: structural heuristics with no LLM calls, embedding candidates with one LLM verification pass, and the full three-pass reasoning above. Each build is dry-run on the live graph, its hypothetical effect rendered in the same delta grammar next to cost and latency badges, and the user adopts the cheapest build that does the job; accepted skills join the agent pool and carry their journal slice as provenance. Skills can contradict one another, one proposing to merge what another would retype. Proposals never auto-apply, and the user can pin a node after checking it by eye: a pinned node is excluded from every agent and skill, and competing proposals on unpinned nodes queue side by side for the user to settle.

\textbf{Retrieval and reasoning.} A query is embedded, matched against node and chunk embeddings, and expanded over the graph into an evidence subgraph; the LLM is prompted over the attached chunk text, so every claim is traceable to a span and streamed with paragraph-level citations. A pluggable Think-on-Graph reasoner~\cite{sun2024thinkongraph} adds LLM-guided multi-hop traversal exposed as a heat overlay, and variants of the same corpus built under different configurations are compared in a synchronized split view.

\textbf{Deployment.} A self-contained batch mode runs without external services; every variant stores its configuration, model versions, and seed for offline audit. All components described in this section are implemented in the released system, which is open source at \url{https://github.com/karpovilia/graphrag}.

\section{Demonstration and Case Studies}
We demonstrate three interactive scenarios; a recorded walkthrough is available at \url{https://vimeo.com/1141894235}.

\textbf{Case Study 1: temporal evolution of a news corpus.} The corpus is a news stream ingested week by week. The activity heatmap shows busy weeks dark and quiet weeks light; scrubbing transaction time replays how the graph grows batch by batch. A two-week brush paints what was born in the window over what persisted, answering what is new this period, and the event-time axis shows point-like publication events against half-open entities. A static visualizer cannot show this: change itself, made legible.

\textbf{Case Study 2: diagnosing cascading errors in a podcast corpus.} The corpus is in Russian, a highly inflective language. A global question yields a fluent answer, but evidence highlighting reveals inflected variants and abbreviations of one department living as separate nodes across communities and distorting retrieval. The user drills from a suspicious entity into its source chunk, merges duplicates, and watches the cascade ripple through the affected communities, the recompute cost reported on a latency badge; re-running the query yields a concise, grounded answer. Across the session the curator performed 62 manual merges and 47 deletions on this graph of roughly 800 nodes; the pattern learner proposes ranked batches of analogous candidates along the way, and compiling the merge slice of the journal into a skill lets the cheap build reproduce the manual merges on a dry run and screen subsequent ingestion batches without further LLM calls, every proposal still arriving as a suggestion. A wrongly invalidated relation is reverted in place.

\textbf{Case Study 3: comparing pipeline variants.} The user builds two variants of the same corpus with different builders or cleaners and asks both the same question. A synchronized split view shows each variant's answer and evidence subgraph side by side; selecting an entity in one pane highlights its counterpart in the other, exposing which configuration yields the better-grounded result.

\textbf{Interactive engagement.} Attendees can scrub and diff any period, inspect the evidence behind an answer, drill from chunks to source text, apply edits and watch the cascade and its latency, review agent suggestions, compile a skill from their own edits and compare its three builds, read the reasoner's traversal heatmap, revert an invalidation, and compare variants side by side.

\textbf{Human evaluation.} Six users worked with the system on the two corpora, issuing over 500 queries, applying edits, and re-running their questions; on a scored subset, answers were rated on factual correctness, coverage, and evidence alignment before and after curation (Table~\ref{tab:graphrag_results_human}); with six raters and no significance testing, the deltas are indicative rather than conclusive. In a seeded error-correction task with six errors per graph and 15 minutes per participant, 78\% of seeded errors were corrected at 0.91 precision, fragmentation errors most reliably. Brief interactive curation restored graph fidelity without retraining.

\section{Limitations}
\textbf{Scalability.} The visualization comfortably handles graphs of up to 30{,}000 nodes, and with type chips and focused views a human rarely needs that many on screen at once. The practical bound today lies elsewhere: compiled skills start to take noticeably long once the active layer they run on exceeds roughly 800 nodes.
\textbf{Dependence on upstream extraction.} The system exposes and helps correct extraction noise but cannot recover information that was never extracted.
\textbf{Temporal granularity.} Event-time forms are assigned structurally (messages as point events, entities as half-open intervals); finer temporal-expression extraction is left to the LLM extractor and is not yet exercised in the demo.
\textbf{Evaluation scope.} The evaluation is demo-oriented and validates qualitative behavior rather than large-scale statistical performance.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
We present GraphCraft, an interactive system that makes change the unit of diagnosis for evolving GraphRAG pipelines. A bi-temporal model and a single delta grammar, rendered across time, edits, and queries and surfaced through an activity-heatmap period scrubber, let users see how an induced graph grows and reorganizes, localize cascading errors, apply persistent edits with immediate, provenance-linked feedback, and correct wrong automatic invalidations. Curation agents propose provenance-linked suggestions, and journal slices compile into skills materialized at three price points and previewed on the live graph. Future work will integrate temporal-expression extraction so facts receive finer event-time bounds automatically, re-compile skills when the curator's behaviour drifts away from them, measure per build tier how many manual curation actions compiled skills save on held-out weeks, arrange these checks into an explicit hierarchy of oracles that spends human attention last, escalating only what structural invariants, journal-induced rules, and LLM verification cannot settle, with conversational schema changes in the spirit of OntoChat~\cite{zhang2024ontochat}, and deepen it with a cross-variant tier in which agreement between independently built graph variants auto-verifies a suggestion, and study temporal multilayer community detection~\cite{mucha2010community} as a way to track how communities persist or split across the corpus timeline.
